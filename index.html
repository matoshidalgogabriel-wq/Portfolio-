Artifact 01 AIML-501 · Workshop 1 Self-Assessment

Leading Change: A Self-Assessment on AI/ML Leadership Readiness

A structured, honest benchmark of my readiness to lead AI/ML initiatives, not just build them, using two formal self-assessment instruments.

Introduction

As part of my M.S. in Artificial Intelligence coursework, I completed two structured self-assessments (one on AI/ML integration leadership skills, one on general change management skills) and reflected on where my instincts as a finance professional already line up with effective AI/ML leadership, and where they don't yet.

Description

Both assessments landed close to what I already suspected about myself, but breaking it into categories sharpened a few things I hadn't put into words before.

AI/ML Integration Leadership: Strengths

  • Stakeholder communication: I spend most weeks translating dashboards and automation logic into something leadership can act on without needing the mechanics.
  • Adoption strategy: I find where automation actually saves time rather than chasing AI because it's trendy. Spotting the cheap, high-impact win comes naturally.
  • Technical understanding: Real fluency, not theoretical: enough bad data problems solved to know what the requirements actually look like in practice.

AI/ML Integration Leadership: Growth Areas

  • Ethics and responsible AI: No formal exposure to bias or regulatory compliance, since everything I've built has been internal tooling, not customer-facing.
  • Change readiness: I've been a builder, not a change manager: no formal training rollout or skills-gap assessment for a team adopting something new.
  • Project management at scale: My projects have stayed scrappy and self-directed, a different skill from managing something with real budget accountability.

Change Management: Strengths

  • Critical thinking, communication, problem-solving: all trace back to the same instinct. Figuring out why numbers don't tie is the same muscle as figuring out why a change initiative isn't landing.

Change Management: Growth Areas

  • Stakeholder engagement at scale: strong one-on-one or in small groups with leadership, but no experience driving org-wide buy-in.
  • Risk management: I tend to solve problems as they surface instead of mapping out failure points ahead of time.
  • Navigating culture across multiple entities: I know one part of the business well, not several moving at once.

Objective

To honestly benchmark my readiness to lead AI/ML initiatives, not just build them, and identify the specific gaps to close before taking on something with broader organizational scope.

Process

I read two articles on change leadership and change-management assessment as prep, then completed both self-assessment forms independently before writing a reflection. Rather than accepting the category scores at face value, I evaluated the assessments themselves as tools: what they measure well, where the scoring logic breaks down, and what I'd revise. Both work as a decent mirror: naming specific skills beats vaguely saying "I'm a good leader." The AI/ML assessment is scoped well for where the field actually is right now. The change management assessment is more exhaustive, and that cuts both ways: sixteen categories is a lot to rate honestly in one sitting, and the weighting feels off, since a category with two questions counts the same as one with six even though they clearly don't carry equal weight in how you read your own results.

If I were revising them, I'd add a category to the AI/ML assessment for evaluating tools and vendors (knowing whether something is worth adopting is a different skill from knowing how to adopt it once you've decided), and I'd fix the weighting on the change management side, either by normalizing the scoring or flagging which categories are core versus supplementary, because right now it distorts how you see your own strengths.

Artifact 02 AIML-501 · Assignment 2.3 Technical Writing / Model Selection

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning: A Case Study Comparison

A written analysis of when to reach for a simple machine learning model and when the problem actually calls for deep learning, argued through two real-world cases.

Introduction

This is a written assignment from AIML-501 (Assignment 2.3). The task was to compare machine learning and deep learning through concrete examples rather than definitions, showing not just what each approach does but when picking one over the other is the right call.

Description

The paper walks through two contrasting cases: customer churn prediction, where a support vector machine is the right tool, and facial recognition, where a convolutional neural network is the right tool. For each, I make the case for why that approach fits and why the other one would fall short.

Case 1: Customer Churn (SVM)

  • Why SVM fits: the data is structured (account age, contract length, monthly charges, support calls) and the features already mean something on their own. A retention manager can see exactly why an account got flagged.
  • Why deep learning would lose: churn datasets run in the tens of thousands of records, not the millions a neural network needs to beat a simpler model. It would also trade a decision that has to be explained for a black box, without enough accuracy gain to justify the loss.

Case 2: Facial Recognition (CNN)

  • Why CNN fits: nobody can hand-write reliable features for a face. A CNN learns its own feature hierarchy straight from raw pixels, starting with edges and textures and building up to full facial structures.
  • Why a simple model would lose: hand-picked measurements throw away almost all the information in the image and break down under a new camera angle, different lighting, or a mask.

Objective

To build the habit of matching model complexity to the actual shape of the data and the business need, instead of defaulting to whichever approach sounds more advanced. Structured, moderately sized data where the reasoning has to be explainable favors a simple model; raw, high-dimensional data where useful features cannot realistically be hand-built favors deep learning.

Process

I started from the course readings on machine learning and deep learning fundamentals, then picked two use cases that sit on opposite ends of the same decision: one where interpretability and a small structured dataset make the classical model the obvious choice, and one where the problem itself (unstructured pixel data with no hand-codable features) rules out anything but a deep network. For each case, I argued both directions: why the chosen model fits, and specifically why the alternative would underperform, rather than just describing what each algorithm does in isolation.

Artifact 03 AIML-501 · Assignment 3.1 Leadership Under Uncertainty

Adapting to Challenges: Leading Through Uncertainty

A leadership reflection on bringing clarity to decisions when the data is incomplete, and on finding the courage to raise an uncomfortable but necessary truth strategically instead of staying silent.

Introduction

This is a written reflection from AIML-501, Assignment 3.1, examining two leadership case studies (Moses and Esther) and applying each to a real challenge in my role: making decisions and communicating judgment calls when I do not yet have every answer.

Description

The reflection works through two situations. The first is about leading when the numbers are incomplete or moving fast: forecasts and dashboards that people act on before every question is answered. The second is about the harder moment of deciding whether to speak up about something uncomfortable, a number that does not tie, a process that is not working, a risk leadership has not fully considered.

Leading Without Full Certainty

  • The instinct to doubt: questioning whether I am positioned to tell the CFO or COO what the numbers mean while still learning the business.
  • The practice instead: slow down enough to ask clarifying questions rather than guess, and stay humble about what is not yet known.
  • Not leading alone: leaning on mentors and colleagues who have been in the seat longer, instead of forcing a polished answer on the spot.

Raising a Hard Truth

  • The default risk: staying silent feels safer in the moment, even when it is not actually the safe choice if the truth needs to be told.
  • The strategic model: plan before acting, build trust first, and pick the timing, instead of raising the issue on emotion alone.
  • The practice: bring the data organized, frame the risk clearly, and have a recommendation ready, so it reads as judgment rather than complaint.

Objective

To build a personal practice for two things that do not show up on a resume: staying grounded and asking good questions when a decision has to be made without complete information, and knowing how to surface a hard finding to leadership deliberately instead of reactively or not at all.

Process

I worked through both assigned case studies against a real challenge I am facing now rather than answering in the abstract. For each, I named the instinct that shows up under pressure first (doubt in one case, silence in the other), then the specific practice I want to replace it with, grounded in something concrete from my actual work: a number that did not tie, a process that was not working, a risk leadership had not fully weighed.

Artifact 04 AIML-501 · Self-Directed AI/ML Tool Evaluation

AI/ML Tool Evaluation Scorecard

A working, weighted scorecard for deciding whether an AI/ML tool is worth adopting, built to close a gap I named in my own Artifact 1 self-assessment.

Open the Live Scorecard →

Introduction

This is a self-directed exercise for AIML-501, not a submitted class assignment. In my Artifact 1 reflection, I wrote that I'd add a category to the AI/ML Integration Leadership Skills Self-Assessment for evaluating tools and vendors, since knowing whether something is worth adopting is a different skill from knowing how to adopt it once you've decided. This artifact is that category, built into an actual working tool instead of left as a suggestion.

Description

The scorecard scores a candidate AI/ML tool across seven weighted criteria on a 1 to 5 scale, then returns a weighted score and a recommendation: Adopt, Pilot First, or Pass.

Heaviest-Weighted Criteria

  • Problem fit (×5): whether the tool solves a real, specific problem, not a solution looking for one.
  • Data readiness (×5): whether the data the tool needs already exists at the volume and quality required.
  • Explainability (×4): whether the output can be explained to the person who has to act on it.

Lighter-Weighted Criteria

  • Vendor and lock-in risk, maintenance burden (×3 each): real constraints, but ones that can be managed after adoption.
  • Cost versus alternative, integration effort (×2 each): solvable constraints, not disqualifiers on their own.
  • Why the split: a tool that is wrong for the problem or the data cannot be fixed with better change management, so those criteria carry more weight than cost or integration friction.

Objective

To turn a gap I identified about myself into something usable: a repeatable way to evaluate whether an AI/ML tool is worth adopting, instead of relying on instinct or a vendor's pitch deck each time.

Process

I started from the seven factors I actually weigh, consciously or not, when I've decided whether to build or adopt something at work, then assigned each a weight based on how hard it is to fix after the fact. Problem fit and data readiness got the highest weights because getting those wrong dooms a project regardless of how well it's managed afterward. Cost and integration effort got the lowest weights because those are usually solvable with time and budget. The score and recommendation update live as the sliders move, so the tool can be used on a real decision, not just read as a description of one.
